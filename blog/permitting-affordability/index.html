Permitting Affordability

Ben Kelly · September 14, 2026 · 6 min read

City leaders often talk of the different factors hurting housing affordability right now: tariffs, cost of labor, and supply chain issues are three of the most cited in my experience. While those three are indeed impacting the cost of housing, they are for the most part out of City Hall’s control. One under-cited cause of the housing crisis is the municipal permit process, sometimes making City Hall its own worst enemy for affordability. As cities struggle to issue timely permits, housing and development in them stagnates.

A recent paper by Leonardo D’Amico, Evan Soltas, and Audrey Wang titled “Where Is Housing Slow to Build, And Is It Getting Slower?¹” examined the permitting process in 60 US cities to find the typical timeline before a project is completed. Their results paint a frustrating picture of cities being slow to respond to the need for more housing.

The worst offenders were coastal cities like New York, Los Angeles, and San Francisco. In those cities, it takes an average of two and a half years for a single family home to be completed, with multi-family units taking four years. While their mayors seek to increase the supply of housing, an apartment that starts development today will take an entire mayoral term before it is complete. Compared to those cities, Sunbelt cities such as Raleigh, Orlando, and Phoenix complete a single family home in a year and a multi-family in two.

I am not aware of any benefits that the residents of Los Angeles and San Francisco receive from these cities’ long permitting processes. The housing built in Raleigh is just as safe as the units built in Los Angeles so it does not seem like the Sunbelt cities are hastily rushing permits through with insufficient oversight.

Disappointingly, even as technology has improved, permitting times have more than doubled over the last 25 years. D’Amico, Soltas, and Wang found that the probability of a multi-family project being approved within six months fell from 71% to 44%. Their study also estimates that a one year increase in permitting duration on average leads to a 39% increase in median rent for the unit. The longer these projects are in limbo, the higher the cost.

This does not have to be the case. Compared to 2000, the technology available to planning departments is miles ahead. Instead of manually looking through a physical zoning code, planners can search through it digitally while looking at properties on Google Maps and GIS systems which should theoretically streamline the process, not slow it down.

I want to be clear that the blame does not rest on the city planners themselves here. They are public servants who are pursuing a complex field where they have to deal with many competing interests. Unfortunately, over the last 25 years, politicians have bogged down municipal zoning codes. They add more mandatory community meetings, stricter zoning rules, more stages of review, and other instances of red tape.

And the politicians themselves most likely did not implement those requirements with the intent of blocking housing, but that is the effect. Each of the regulations was made with good intentions, but good intentions do not automatically produce affordability. As more and more red tape is added, fewer homes get built and housing gets more expensive.

As a Democrat, I am frustrated with all these blue cities failing to meet the moment on housing. States like California and New York are losing population to Florida and Texas, and a major reason is that those are states that are building housing and getting prices down.

If Democrats want to get in power to implement their preferred policies, we need to show that Democrats know how to govern so people stop moving away from these blue coastal states to the red Sunbelt states. In the 2030 congressional reapportionment, Texas and Florida are estimated to gain 3 or 4 seats each while California is projected to lose up to four. That means more congressional representation for those states and more power in the electoral college. To grow the population in these blue states, we need to get serious about reform.

It feels odd to be a Democrat advocating for cutting regulations and red tape. Often when I call for speeding up government or reducing regulations, my progressive friends will joke that I am secretly a Republican, but the reality is that I believe in the power and promise of government, and I recognize that we need to have an effective City Hall to show that Democrats can govern. Right now cities are burying themselves in red tape and are losing residents as a result.

The good news is that plenty of municipalities have noticed these problems as well and are looking for solutions. The most basic is simplifying the zoning code. Legislators have added more and more requirements to these codes while rarely removing any regulations. This results in these unwieldy zoning codes that confuse even experienced city planners. Burbank, California, streamlined its zoning code and removed certain requirements in 2022, with city officials estimating that the changes could cut their permitting time in half².

Some cities have created accountability systems to show their commitment to speeding up the process. Denver implemented a 180-day “shot clock” for permitting, guaranteeing a city response to permit requests within that timeframe. If the city fails to do so, the permit applicant is entitled to up to $10k in fee refunds. San Diego chose a more focused approach called Affordable Housing Permit Now (AHPN): a 30-day “shot clock” for income-restricted affordable housing. While Denver’s shot clock was just implemented in 2025 and has little public data, San Diego’s model has been a proven success. According to the city, AHPN has resulted in 2,848 completed affordable homes, 1,589 homes under construction and 2,809 homes currently in the review process and has also supported 387 emergency shelter beds³.

Of course, cities must have the capacity to get these permits done correctly. Simplifying the code helps, but an even faster route chosen by some cities is to implement AI in permitting review.

Honolulu and Seattle are two cities that have done so and experienced positive results. Both cities contracted with CivCheck, a platform that examines applications and compares them with a city’s zoning code to check for completeness. Applicants upload their forms into CivCheck’s system, and they receive a near-instant reply telling them if the application is complete or not. After a completed review is uploaded, it goes to the human reviewers in the city department for final review. Honolulu has a long-term contract while Seattle ran a pilot and is now pursuing a longer-term test after the success of the initial term. The state of California arranged for a different AI permitting vendor called Archistar to help process applications for rebuilding after the 2025 wildfires.

While it is still a new technology, AI permitting has so far shown to be a success for both cities and applicants. It sped up the permitting process and helped housing get built faster. As more and more housing breaks ground through these faster permits, residents will start seeing cheaper housing and more vibrant cities.

I have heard from others in city government worried that this technology would result in city planners being laid off. That result strikes me as unlikely. I do not know a single city planner who says that they are underworked or they like doing simple completeness checks on applications. This technology instead allows the planner to focus more on the complex and technical elements of the applications while the basic yet time-consuming tasks are done by AI.

Even if one is against the use of AI in permitting review, hopefully all can agree that cities benefit from reduced permitting times. These backlogs make housing more expensive and prevent cities from growing, but the good news is that the solution is entirely within City Hall’s control. If leaders prioritize permitting, they can make real progress towards affordability.
