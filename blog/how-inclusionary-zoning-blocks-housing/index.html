How Inclusionary Zoning Blocks Housing

Ben Kelly · July 20, 2026 · 5 min read

Some of the worst ideas in government are the ones that sound good on paper. One (possibly apocryphal) example is during British rule of colonial Delhi, in an attempt to bring down the cobra population, the government offered a cash bounty for every dead cobra. This led to scores of people bringing in dead cobras for the bounty. Sounds like a good idea, problem solved, right?

Unfortunately, the bounty system led to people breeding cobras just to kill them for the bounty. When the British heard about this, they ended the bounty system and the cobra breeders released all of their cobras, creating an even larger population of cobras than before the bounty system. In their attempt to cull the cobras, the colonial government set up a system that created even more cobras.

While not as deadly, America has a somewhat similar counterintuitive system to solve a problem. The US has a massive shortage of affordable housing, so on paper it sounds like a good idea to require that a certain percentage of new housing be rented at an affordable price. Many cities throughout the country have implemented these Inclusionary Zoning (IZ) rules to force developers to include more affordable units. This is a worthy goal, but similar to the cobra bounties, in practice it is counterintuitive and can make the problem worse according to a new paper released last month from UC Irvine.

Before I worked in government, I looked at all the housing developments being approved by the local government and thought to myself “Why don’t they just require that a certain percentage of the housing has cheaper rent? That way developers would be forced to produce affordable housing.” I did not know I was just describing Inclusionary Zoning. Unfortunately, the quick and easy solution rarely pans out in practice.

In Noah Kouchekinia’s study “Inclusionary Zoning and Housing Supply: Evidence from California’s Palmer Fix¹”, the author examines the effects of IZ in California, which had a unique situation that made it a good test case for IZ. A 2009 court ruling, Palmer v. LA, effectively struck down IZ in California, labeling it as an illegal form of rent control, until 2017 when the state legislature updated the state code with a so-called “Palmer Fix” to allow IZ again. This 8 year period gives a clear before-and-after of IZ and its effects on housing production as IZ enforcement was suddenly cut off in 2009 before resuming in 2017.

I would recommend people read the full study to get a better understanding of its methodology, but in short it looked at the various forms of IZ in California cities and assigned it a rating based on the strictness of the policy and examined how much rent a landlord would have to forgo in the city. In many studies, researchers use a binary “Has IZ / Does Not Have IZ” designation, but the strictness of the IZ policy is critical. For example, a requirement that 20% of new units be affordable causes more foregone rent than a 10% requirement. The definition of “affordable” is also important, with some localities having it set at a lower income level than others.

This measure of stringency is important when people point to cities like Minneapolis as a positive example of IZ. Minneapolis has been able to produce a large number of units over the last few years while having an IZ policy, but Minneapolis’s policy is fairly minor. It only applies to developments with more than 20 units and requires only 8% of the development to be moderately affordable. In contrast, Santa Monica in 1998 enacted a requirement that 30% of all new housing, even in smaller developments, be rented at even more affordable levels than Minneapolis. Purely on paper, it sounds like Santa Monica should have cheaper housing, but in practice it is the opposite. For research purposes, it is essential to acknowledge the difference between Minneapolis and Santa Monica’s housing policies.

Kouchekinia’s study measured how much rent was being foregone due to these IZ policies. For example, if there were no IZ policies in place, then a landlord would rent every unit at market rate. With an IZ ordinance, they would have to rent a certain number of units at a lower rate. Under Santa Monica’s rules, if a new building has 50 units and market rent is $2,000/month each ($100,000/month total if fully market rate), the city requires 30% of units (15 units) to be affordable to low-income tenants, meaning rent capped at $1,200/month for those units instead of $2,000. That’s $12,000/month in forgone rent across the building ($800 x 15), against the $100,000/month market-rate baseline. The landlord will either have to eat the cost of that $12,000/month, or they will charge the other units approximately $350 more per month to make up for it. While that is good news for those 15 people who were lucky enough to get the IZ units, it means that all the other tenants are paying more to subsidize those units. That is also only if they can find tenants at that higher rate. If they are charging over 10% higher than market rate for the units, the landlord will have trouble filling those units.

One could make a moral argument that they are fine with the wealthier tenants subsidizing the rent for the lower income residents, but Kouchekinia’s study determined that IZ actually reduces the amount of housing that is built in a city. In his examination of 575 different California localities, the author determined that for every additional percentage point of rent foregone under an IZ rule, new housing permits are reduced by about 7.6%. Of the 575 California jurisdictions examined in the study, the average rent being foregone in areas with IZ was 4.2%, meaning that California’s typical IZ ordinance reduced the number of units being built by almost 32%.

Kouchekinia found that renters in IZ jurisdictions in California paid about $6.97 billion in additional rent due to the IZ policies which produced 8,990 low-income units during that window. On average, each affordable unit cost $775,000 in excess rent paid by the other renters. The author points out that this is far higher than the cost of subsidizing low-income housing through the Low Income Housing Tax Credit program (LIHTC), which has an average marginal cost in California of $441,000. In addition, while the cost of LIHTC is paid for by the government, the affordable unit subsidy from IZ is paid for by other renters. It should be noted that on average, renters already have lower income than homeowners, so it is not like the mega rich are the ones paying under IZ.

This study adds to the growing body of literature and data showing the dangers of IZ. While the policymakers may have had good intentions, like the creators of the cobra bounties, they have just made the problem worse.
