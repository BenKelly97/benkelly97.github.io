The Case for Parking Tickets

Ben Kelly · June 18, 2026 · 4 min read

The Case for Parking Tickets

Nobody likes walking back to their car and seeing that little white sheet on the windshield meaning they will have to pay $65 for a parking ticket. I have been in that situation multiple times, and I can promise you I was never happy about it. But I also know that it is a necessary part of life in an organized society, and that if we did not have parking tickets, life in the city would be worse.

Disliking tickets but saying they make life better may sound contradictory, but I think it is the logical conclusion. I believe consistent enforcement of parking rules in a city is necessary for three reasons: revenue, equity, and quality of life.

Cities raise a significant amount of money through parking tickets and fines, with my home city of Cincinnati collecting $4.1 million in fines in 2025 (or roughly $13 per resident). Cincinnati has a total budget of almost $600 million, so while it is not one of the city’s primary funding mechanisms, it does bring in real money.

Of course, Cincinnati is not alone in collecting these fines. New York City brings in over $500 million each year in parking tickets ($63 per resident), and Milwaukee collected $17 million ($30 per resident) in 2025.

If we stopped collecting tickets, the government would have to either raise taxes or cut spending to make up for that. Perhaps in Cincinnati that would be $4 million less of roads repaved in a year, or less frequent trash pickup, or maybe they would have to raise fees for summer camps or other city programs. To me, forgiving illegal parking is not worth the financial tradeoff.

When cars are parked illegally, it can hurt people. While I am walking around downtown, if there is a car on the sidewalk I can simply walk around it. But what if I were a blind person or a wheelchair user? Does the driver’s desire to park wherever they want trump the disabled person’s right to move freely through the city sidewalks?

Cars are often ticketed for parking in bus stops. A delay in the bus schedule might not be the end of the world, but it would disrupt the lives of many people, particularly lower income or working class individuals who use the bus for transportation. Does the driver’s desire to find an easy parking spot trump their right to use reliable public transportation?

When it comes to fires, every second counts. The reason that cars are ticketed for parking in front of hydrants is that they are directly blocking a firefighter from doing their job. This is a real problem, such as in January when the New York Fire Department were delayed from responding to a five-alarm fire because the three closest fire hydrants all had cars parked in front of them¹. Does the driver’s desire to spend less time looking for parking trump people’s right to safety?

When I go to the grocery store, I always return my shopping cart to the corral. I do this even though it might be a bit out of my way or it is raining or I am in a hurry, and even though I know that the damage of leaving my cart out would be minimal. But at the same time, if everyone left the cart out, the grocery store would be pure chaos. It works the same with parking.

If one person parks illegally, it is a minor inconvenience to the neighborhood. If twenty people park illegally, it could pose a serious disruption. By telling people that parking rules do not matter, we are saying those disruptions do not matter. Increasing enforcement of parking rules does improve compliance and increases the quality of life for citizens. In New York City, after the city began consistently enforcing rules against parking in bus lanes, residents complied and bus speeds increased by 30%².

As I stated earlier, nobody ever likes getting these tickets, and it is true that a $60-or-so fine can be a major hassle, especially to someone who is already living paycheck-to-paycheck. Some cities have implemented tiered fine structures, where lower income residents pay a smaller fine, such as in DC where SNAP recipients can apply for a 50% reduction in ticket fines for speeding³. I think that is a brilliant system and I would encourage cities to look into it and even expand it to cover parking tickets, but there must still be a fine issued to discourage illegal parking. If we are afraid of enforcing the law because people find it inconvenient, then we might as well stop enforcing the speed limit or DUI laws because it might hurt a driver’s wallet, nevermind the real harm this would cause to society.

I understand the desire to waive or limit ticket fees because of worries over cost of living and the economy, but before cities consider doing so, they must think about the cost. As I have shown in this piece, the cost is not just lost revenue for the government, but lost time for commuters, lost mobility for the disabled, and lost safety for residents.
