How an Obscure Ohio Law Increases Healthcare Costs

Ben Kelly · June 29, 2026 · 6 min read

Tooth decay hits the poor and vulnerable the hardest. Children from low income families are twice as likely to have cavities as their wealthier counterparts¹. This disparity also hurts the disabled and those in rural communities who will have a harder time finding care.

For those with insurance, a cavity may just mean an annoying visit to the dentist where they get a filling. But for those who cannot get treatment, that cavity will cause them chronic pain and eventually lead to much worse health outcomes and potentially even lead to death.

It is recommended that people go to the dentist every six months for a professional cleaning and a checkup. The vast majority of the time is spent on the teeth cleaning by a dental hygienist, only for the dentist to make a brief appearance at the end and briefly look around before concluding the visit for a typical cost upwards of $100.

As people look for ways to increase affordability and access to healthcare, I started looking into how oral healthcare could become cheaper for the average family. After looking at the existing systems and many studies, I believe there are structural barriers that the state of Ohio has implemented that are reducing access to dental care simply because of political connections and the influence of money in politics.

It is clear that getting more people to have regular cleanings will have a positive effect on public health, and there are certain unnecessary regulatory barriers right now increasing the cost of cleanings. One major piece of red tape in Ohio law is the requirement that dental hygienists primarily work in a dental practice with the dentist physically in the building. While this may not seem like a big deal, it has major consequences and prevents some vital work from being performed.

Even though the person doing most of the work during a teeth cleaning is a dental hygienist who makes an average of $94k per year, you are also paying for the dentist who makes at least double and often triple that. The dentist is doing little-to-no work during a cleaning, but the state of Ohio requires their presence which raises the cost considerably.

Not every state requires this. Colorado, Maine, New Mexico, and Washington allow hygienists to own and operate their own clinics offering teeth cleaning services, which gives people of those states a lower cost choice for teeth cleaning. It also allows hygienists more freedom to perform charitable services, which one dental hygienist named Anita Rodriguez did in Washington state for more than 20 years. Her independent practice, Elder Care Dental Hygiene, provided low cost dental cleaning in a mobile clinic for seniors and those with special needs. She also trained other hygienists to perform these good works, further increasing access to oral health care for those in need².

In addition to helping with charitable cases, it also gives hygienists more career opportunities and the ability to open up their own independent practice. This encourages more people to pursue a career as hygienists as it would increase the salary range and offer more flexibility.

A dental hygienist in Ohio only receives their license after completing more than 1,000 hours of supervised clinical practice, passing the National Board Dental Hygiene Examination, passing a separate regional clinical examination, and passing the Ohio Dental Practice Act jurisprudence exam. After all of that training though, the state of Ohio still says they need supervision and cannot operate independently as dental hygienists.

The only time that Ohio allows a hygienist to operate independently is through the Ohio Oral Health Access Supervision Program. This program allows hygienists to perform work independently in certain facilities³ to increase access to care. While this is a good step, the fine print shows that it still heavily restricts hygienists. To perform this charitable work, the hygienist needs a permit signed by a dentist that they work for and said dentist can authorize no more than three hygienists for this type of work. In addition, if they perform cleaning services for anyone, that individual must go to a dentist before a hygienist is allowed to clean for them again⁴. This shows that while the legislature does trust hygienists to work without supervision, it only does so when they will not receive significant compensation for their work.

If Anita Rodriguez from Washington tried to perform her mobile dental care work in Ohio, she would have had to work full time for a dentist, receive written permission before going to any facility to perform cleaning services, and would only be able to help a patient once and would not be able to return to them until that patient has seen a dentist. She also would not have been able to train other hygienists to carry on this work, further limiting access to dental care.

It is worth noting the gender aspect of this. Almost 95% of dental hygienists are women, but they make up less than 40% of dentists. This is a male dominated field of dentists saying that this woman dominated field of hygienists cannot be trusted to work without supervision.

When I see a situation like this, I look at what the potential barriers are to changing it — who is content with the current system and would dislike it changing, and what connections that group has.

Right now, this system greatly benefits dentists, specifically dentists who own their own practices. This means they do not have to compete with hygienists opening up their own cleaning clinics, which could deprive them of employees and increase their competition.

To be clear, I do not think the American Dental Association (ADA) is a nefarious organization secretly trying to give poor people unhealthy teeth. The truth is they are an organization whose primary goal is to support the interests of dentists, and if a dentist has to compete with independent hygienist clinics, they will have more competition for their services and may have a harder time hiring staff. It makes sense that the ADA fights against changes that may impact the financial bottom line.

Unfortunately, the system breaks down when one side is significantly more powerful than the other. The dental lobby is particularly powerful compared to the dental hygienist lobby. The ADA, which opposes independent teeth cleaning clinics, spent $1.84 million on lobbying in 2024 and contributed $2.5 million to campaigns⁵. Meanwhile, the American Dental Hygienist Association (ADHA), which states that hygienists should be able to operate independently, spends a negligible amount lobbying, and I am not able to find any PAC contributions on their behalf⁶. They are not funding politicians, which means they are not in the room with politicians when these rules are made.

State level lobbying is harder to track, but the Ohio Dental Association’s (ODA) website speaks of a daily presence at the Ohio Statehouse and says that 90% of their supported candidates won their races. Much of the work the ODA does is admirable, such as in 2023 when it helped convince legislators to almost double the funding for Ohio’s dental Medicaid program⁷. The ODA also has tremendous influence through the Ohio Dental Board, which is made up of nine dentists, three dental hygienists, and one unaffiliated member. The governor appoints members to the board after their names are put forward by the ODA and the Ohio Dental Hygienist Association (ODHA).

This gives the dentist lobby even more power, as the ODA is essentially choosing the people that will be making the rules regulating dentists and they control almost three quarters of the board.

The dental lobby has much more influence and money than the hygienist lobby, and the Ohio Dental Board is not going to recommend an action like this that increases competition for dentists. The only hope is that this can become a bipartisan issue where determined lawmakers of both parties work together to change the system.

This is not a right-or-left issue. It is not a liberal or a conservative move to give dental hygienists more freedom in their career options, it is just an evidence-based move to improve people’s health outcomes. The ADA and ODA should be consulted on all legislation related to dental care, but they should not be given complete control of the rules and regulations. Just because hygienists have fewer political connections does not mean they should need a dentists’ permission to clean teeth.
